# Design Patterns Quests: Outcomes, Load, and Sources

This series covers three of the most common design patterns in game programming: **State**, **Observer**, and **Singleton**. 

In software, engineers keep running into the same problems over and over. So they work out solutions worth reusing and share them as best practices. That is what a design pattern is: a named, reusable solution to a problem that shows up again and again.

A good pattern gives you two things:

- **A known solution**, so you don't reinvent one every time.
- **A shared vocabulary**, so you can describe your solution to another engineer in a word or two. Say "I made it a state machine" and they already know the shape of what you built.
## Learning outcomes

- You can recognize and implement common design patterns like State, singleton and observer. 
- You can identify signs of growing complexity in a codebase, such as deeply nested conditionals or tightly coupled systems, and propose a design pattern to address them.
- You can evaluate whether introducing a design pattern is justified for a given problem or whether a simpler solution is more appropriate.
- You can use the vocabulary of common design patterns to communicate architectural ideas and trade-offs with other developers.

## Sources

- **[Level up your code with design patterns and SOLID](https://unity.com/resources/design-patterns-solid-ebook)** (Unity's free e-book). Covers implementing the patterns using unity best practices. 
- **[Game Programming Patterns](https://gameprogrammingpatterns.com/)** One of the most well-known resources for design patterns in game development. Uses C++ for its examples, but the real value is in the opinionated, context-rich explanations of when and why each pattern is useful. Also available free to read online.
- **[Refactoring Guru: Design Patterns](https://refactoring.guru/design-patterns)**. Best used as a quick reference or a refresher on what a pattern is about. Language agnostic.
